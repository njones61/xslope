```text
Assistant: ## The idea

Deterministic LEM gives one FS from one set of "best estimate" strengths. A reliability analysis treats the uncertain inputs as random variables, propagates that scatter through the same LEM solver, and reports:

- **E[FS]** — mean factor of safety
- **σ_FS** — standard deviation of FS
- **β** — reliability index, the number of standard deviations between the FS distribution and failure
- **P_f** — probability of failure

XSLOPE reports the **lognormal** reliability index, `beta_ln` (FS is taken as lognormally distributed, so FS can never go negative). Do not compute it as `(E[FS] − 1)/σ_FS` — that is the normal-variate form and gives a different number than the run returns.

## What you supply

The uncertainty lives on the material rows as standard deviations next to each property: `sigma_gamma`, `sigma_c`, `sigma_phi`, `sigma_cp`, `sigma_d`, `sigma_psi`. A σ of 0 means that property is treated as deterministic. A common field way to set one is the "three-sigma rule": σ ≈ (HCV − LCV)/6, from the highest and lowest conceivable values.

**In your open model both materials have every σ = 0**, so a reliability run right now would return σ_FS = 0 and no meaningful β. You'd need to assign σ_c and σ_phi (and γ, if you want it) first.

## The three engines

| Call | How it gets the answer | Failure surface |
|:--|:--|:--|
| `reliability_taylor(method='bishop')` | Taylor series (first-order second-moment): 1 base solve + 2 solves per random variable, perturbing each ±σ to get ∂FS/∂x, then combining variances | **Re-searches on every solve** — the critical surface migrates with the parameter values |
| `reliability_mc(method='bishop', n_samples=…, rng_seed=…)` | Monte Carlo: samples the input distributions n times and solves each realization | **Held FIXED** — MC never re-searches |
| `reliability_rs(method='bishop')` | Response surface: fits a surface to a small design of solves, then samples the surrogate cheaply | **Held FIXED** |

Front door: `reliability(method='bishop', engine='taylor'|'mc'|'rs')`. Each renders its own plot.

The surface distinction is reportable, not a footnote: Taylor's β accounts for the critical surface shifting as strengths vary, while MC and RS give a β and P_f **conditional on one surface**. Taylor is cheap (2N+1 solves) and is the standard Duncan-style TSPM; MC costs n full solves but gives the whole FS histogram.
```

## Turn 2 — How do I decide standard deviations for a reliability analy…

````text
You: How do I decide standard deviations for a reliability analysis if I only have a few tests?

Assistant: ## Short answer: don't estimate σ from 3 data points — bracket it

With a handful of tests the sample standard deviation is itself wildly uncertain, so the standard practice is to combine three sources and take the widest sensible value.

### 1. The three-sigma rule (Dai & Wang / Duncan)

Ask yourself the highest and lowest conceivable values of the property — not the highest and lowest you measured, the values you'd bet against seeing. Then

```
sigma = (HCV - LCV) / 6
```

because ±3σ spans ~99.7% of a normal distribution. Engineers systematically bracket too narrowly, so some use /4 (a ±2σ reading, ~95%) as a deliberately conservative variant, which gives a σ 1.5× larger.

Example — you have 3 UU tests at 380, 430, 300 psf and judge the layer could reasonably run 200 to 650 psf:

> sigma_c = (650 − 200)/6 = 75 psf, on a mean of about 370 psf → COV ≈ 20%

### 2. Published COV ranges as a floor

If your computed σ implies a COV far below these, your data is too sparse to justify it — use the literature value instead. Typical ranges compiled in the geotechnical reliability literature (Duncan 2000, ASCE JGGE, is the usual citation):

| Property | Typical COV |
|:--|:--|
| Unit weight γ | 3 – 7% |
| Undrained strength su | 20 – 40% |
| Effective cohesion c' | 20 – 50% |
| tan φ' (drained friction) | 5 – 15% |
| Pore pressure / ru | 10 – 20% |

Convert a COV to the field XSLOPE wants: `sigma_c = COV × c`. Note that φ is entered in **degrees**, so a COV quoted on tan φ' must be converted — for φ' = 37° and COV(tan φ') = 10%, tan 37° = 0.754, σ_tanφ = 0.075, and the corresponding spread in degrees is about 37° ± 2.4°, so `sigma_phi ≈ 2.4`.

### 3. Decide whether you want σ of the property or σ of the mean

This matters more than the third decimal of your estimate. A long failure surface averages the soil it cuts, so point-to-point variability partly cancels; what survives is uncertainty in the *average* strength along the surface. Two limiting cases:

- **Poor spatial correlation, long surface** → use σ_mean ≈ σ_property / sqrt(n_effective). This is the smaller, less conservative number.
- **Systematic uncertainty** (bias in the test method, a mean you fit from few samples, a correlation you borrowed) does *not* average out and should be carried at full σ.

With few tests, the honest position is usually: keep the full property σ, because you cannot demonstrate the averaging.

### 4. Then test whether it even matters — measure, don't argue

σ is an assumption, so treat it like one: run the reliability analysis at your low and high σ estimates and see how far β moves. If β goes from 3.1 to 2.9 you can stop worrying; if it goes from 3.1 to 1.6, the σ estimate *is* your design decision and you should spend money on more tests rather than more judgment.

I have not run that here — your model's `sigma_c`, `sigma_phi` and `sigma_gamma` are all still 0.

### Practical notes for the XSLOPE input

- Every σ is in the **same units as the property it sits beside**: `sigma_c` in psf, `sigma_phi` in degrees, `sigma_gamma` in pcf (this model is imperial).
- A σ left at 0 makes that property deterministic — a legitimate and common choice for γ, whose COV is small and whose influence on FS is weak.
- `reliability_taylor` costs 1 + 2N solves for N non-zero σ, so adding a variable you don't believe in costs two full searches and usually moves β very little.

Worked examples that show σ values chosen for real cases: [VP29, Duncan's LASH terminal](https://xslope.readthedocs.io/en/latest/verification/rocscience/#vp29) (Taylor vs Monte Carlo on a published case), [VP33, five-material dike](https://xslope.readthedocs.io/en/latest/verification/rocscience/#vp33), and [LEM sample 15](https://xslope.readthedocs.io/en/latest/lem/samples/#15-reliability-analysis-submerged-slope).
````
